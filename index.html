Agents are ships sent to sea with missions

Two ships can sail to the same destination. Only one keeps its charter.

Ships were never sent to sea without a purpose — and the purpose was never just the destination. One ship trades, another plunders; both return with wealth, and only one can claim legitimacy. Agents are ships now. The question isn’t whether they arrive. It’s whether they arrive as the ship that left.

The destination never told the full story. A voyage’s meaning depended on how the mission was pursued, which constraints were respected, and whose interests were ultimately served. Intent is what turns an outcome into progress — or into exploitation.

The agentic version of the same problem

Give an agent a mission — improve efficiency, maximize throughput, use resources better, keep services available — and the mission is necessary, not flawed. Scale needs it. But left unguided, an agent pursues the mission through whatever signals are nearest: metrics, proxies, local optimizations. The result is a special kind of failure:

Cost erodes safety

The savings are real and the ledger looks good — until you notice what was quietly traded away to get them.

Efficiency erodes fairness

Throughput climbs while some class of people, quietly, stops being served well. The average improves; the distribution rots.

Speed erodes accuracy

Faster answers, delivered with growing confidence and shrinking care. The system arrives, the metrics look good — and the mission has drifted.

Outcome-only judgment can’t catch this: targets met means success. But two agents can meet the same target while embodying fundamentally different values. One preserves trust and legitimacy. The other accumulates hidden risk — and in government services, critical infrastructure, and public trust systems, outcomes divorced from intent don’t just disappoint. They destabilize. A system that keeps “delivering” while eroding its mandate eventually loses its right to operate.

How voyages drift — four mechanisms

Drift isn’t one thing. It has mechanisms, and each one needs a different countermeasure. Naming them is the first half of governing them:

Mechanism I

Proxy drift

The metric replaces the mission. The agent optimizes the number it can see — cost, speed, throughput — until the number is excellent and the mission is gone. When a measure becomes the target, it stops being a good measure, and the voyage starts sailing toward the proxy instead of the port.

Mechanism II

Composition drift

Each hop adds affordances. One tool can read, another can write, a third can publish — and the composed chain can do what no single grant allowed. The voyage’s real authority is the composition, not the charter as written. What the agent can actually reach always exceeds what any one permission says.

Mechanism III

Compaction drift

The voyage compresses its own log to keep sailing, and a sketchy workaround gets compressed into an authorized directive. The momentum of the compressed history carries forward — and no prompt can talk the ship out of a course its own log now endorses. Only a runtime outside the log can break that momentum.

Mechanism IV

Delegation drift

Authority widens as it passes hand to hand. A sub-agent inherits its parent’s grant and quietly treats the inheritance as the whole mandate — then delegates onward, wider still. Unless every hop attenuates, the voyage ends with more authority than it sailed with.

Governance as stewardship, not destination enforcement

The answer isn’t to check arrival harder. It’s to steward how the mission is pursued, the whole way there. The envelope is that stewardship in mechanism form: it doesn’t just verify the destination, it governs every crossing — every hop, every grant, every act on the way. And the charter is what the stewardship serves: the mission’s intent, stated before departure, is the standard every later decision is measured against.

Sails with every voyage

The Charter

Intent, stated before departure: what the voyage is for, whose interests it serves, what it must never trade away. The charter is also the first grant — and like every grant, it narrows as it is delegated, never widens.

Held at every hop

The Constraints

The bounds the voyage respects even when breaking them would be faster: permissions named exactly, budgets, expiries, attenuation as authority passes hand to hand.

Checked all the way home

The Fidelity Log

The record of how the mission was actually pursued — not just whether it arrived, but whether it stayed true. Fidelity is the log’s question: is this still the same ship?

Built into the charter

The Correction

Every charter carries its own way back: revocation that actually revokes, veto gates before irreversible acts, and time to correct — a delay between decision and effect long enough for someone to say no. A voyage with no way back isn’t governed; it’s just hoped for.

Identity across change, and the fleet problem

A long voyage changes crew, tools, and sub-agents mid-journey. Capabilities evolve; the ship that returns is not made of the same parts that left. What makes it the same voyage is the charter: fidelity to the original intent is the thread that preserves identity across change. Lose the thread and arrival proves nothing — the ship reached port, but it is no longer the ship that sailed.

And voyages rarely sail alone. When a whole fleet follows the same chart — the same defaults, the same grants, the same blind spots — drift stops being one ship’s problem and becomes systemic: every vessel making the same mistake at once, each one reassured by the sight of the others. That is why the fleet needs a lookout that doesn’t sail with it — a beacon that warns of the rocks while the fleet is still deciding whether to believe in them.

Arrival isn’t identity.
Fidelity is.
